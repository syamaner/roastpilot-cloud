# Roast curve phases: a plain explainer

A roast curve is simply a plot of bean temperature against time. Reading one is
mostly a matter of recognising a handful of phases that every batch passes
through, whatever the roaster or the origin. The phases below are descriptive,
not prescriptive; they name what tends to happen, and roasters read them the way
a sailor reads weather rather than the way an accountant reads a ledger.

## Charge and the turning point

The roast begins at charge, the moment green beans hit the hot drum. Bean
temperature drops sharply at first, because the room-temperature beans pull heat
out of the drum faster than the burner can replace it. The lowest point of that
dip is the turning point, and it usually arrives somewhere in the first minute.
After the turning point the beans start to gain heat again, and the curve begins
its long climb.

The depth of the turning point depends mostly on charge mass and charge
temperature. A heavier charge pulls the temperature lower and takes longer to
recover; a hotter drum at charge softens the dip. Neither is better in the
abstract. They are levers, and different beans reward different settings. Many
roasters keep a log of turning points across batches, because a turning point
that drifts from its usual place is often the first sign that something upstream
has changed, a colder room, a damper hopper, a heavier scoop.

## The drying phase

For roughly the first third of the roast the beans are mostly shedding moisture.
Green coffee arrives with a good deal of water in it, and that water has to leave
before the more interesting reactions can take hold. During this stretch the
beans stay a pale, grassy green and then drift toward yellow. The smell in the
room is often described as bready or hay-like.

Drying is slow and forgiving compared with what follows. The curve tends to be
smooth and steady here, and small adjustments to airflow or heat have gentle,
gradual effects. Rush the drying phase and you risk scorching the outside of the
bean while the inside is still damp, which shows up later as a baked, flat cup.
Take it too slowly and the beans can stew, picking up a dull, muted quality that
no amount of care later in the roast will fully recover.

## The Maillard phase

Once the beans dry out and warm past roughly 150 Celsius, the Maillard reactions
take over. This is the browning stage, where sugars and amino acids recombine
into hundreds of new aromatic compounds. The beans turn from yellow to tan to a
light brown, and the aroma shifts from bready toward something closer to toast
and roasted nuts.

Maillard is where a lot of a coffee's sweetness and body is built, so roasters
tend to watch the pace here closely. A curve that flattens too much can produce a
dull, papery flavour; a curve that climbs too steeply can rush past the sweetness
and leave the cup thin. The art is keeping the temperature rising steadily but
never stalling.

## Reading the rate of rise

Many roasters plot a second line alongside the temperature curve: the rate of
rise, which is just how fast the bean temperature is climbing at any moment.
Where the temperature curve tells you where you are, the rate of rise tells you
where you are heading. A healthy roast usually shows a rate of rise that falls
gently and smoothly through the roast, never rising back up and never dropping to
a flat line.

A flat line in the rate of rise is the one shape almost everyone tries to avoid.
It signals a stall, a stretch where the beans have stopped gaining heat, and a
stall near first crack is widely blamed for baked, lifeless flavours. Reading the
rate of rise is largely about spotting trouble a little before it shows up in the
temperature itself, which is why so many roasters trust it more than the raw
curve.

## First crack

The most dramatic landmark on the curve is first crack. As internal pressure
builds, the beans fracture audibly, a sound a lot like popcorn or snapping
twigs. First crack marks the start of the roast's development window, the stretch
where flavour is set and the difference between a light and a dark roast is
decided.

The curve often shows a small wobble around first crack, because the crack
releases moisture and energy in a short burst. Experienced roasters expect that
wobble and read through it rather than chasing it with the burner.

## Development and drop

After first crack comes development time, the interval between the first audible
crack and the moment the beans are dropped from the drum. Short development
tends to preserve brighter, more acidic notes; longer development trades some of
that brightness for deeper, rounder, more roast-forward flavours. Push far
enough and you reach second crack, where the beans fracture again and the roast
tips toward dark and smoky.

Drop is the end of the story, the point where the beans tumble into the cooling
tray and the curve stops. A good roaster judges drop by a blend of colour, smell,
sound, and the shape of the curve itself, rather than by any single number.

## Cooling and consistency

The roast is not quite finished at drop. Beans hold a lot of heat, and if they
sit in a warm pile they keep roasting from the inside, so most roasters rush them
into a cooling tray with a strong draught of air pulled through them. The faster
the beans reach room temperature, the more faithfully the cup reflects the
choices made during the roast rather than the slow drift of residual heat.

Consistency, in the end, is the quiet goal behind all of this. A single good
roast is a happy accident; a repeatable one is a craft. Roasters chase
repeatability by holding the early phases steady, by watching the rate of rise
for the first hint of a stall, and by dropping on the same combination of cues
batch after batch. The curve is a record of choices, and reading it well is
mostly a habit of paying attention to how each phase flows into the next.
